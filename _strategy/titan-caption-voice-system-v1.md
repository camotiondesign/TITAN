# Titan Caption Voice System v1

**Status:** Live writing manual for Titan PMR + Titanverse social content  
**Date:** 21 August 2026  
**Audience:** Human writers, AI writers, editors, content agents and QA agents  
**Relationship to strategy:** This manual sits beneath the Titan Content Operating Strategy. The strategy decides what deserves to be published. This manual decides how that idea should sound when it becomes copy. Where older caption-writing guidance conflicts with this document, use this document.

---

## 1. Purpose

Titan should not sound as though every post has passed through the same copywriting machine.

The goal is not to create a single rigid “Titan voice”. The goal is to create writing that feels recognisably human, specific to pharmacy, appropriate to the situation and varied enough that the audience cannot reverse-engineer the formula behind it.

The central rule is:

> **The framework should shape the writing, but the reader should never be able to see the framework.**

The Reason-to-Care Markers remain the content gate:

- **Craft** — worth watching
- **Value** — worth knowing
- **Connection** — worth caring about
- **Proof** — worth believing or wanting

These markers determine whether an idea is worth publishing. They are not a caption template.

---

## 2. The first writing question

Do not begin with:

> “What is the key message?”

Begin with:

> **“What is the most interesting true thing here?”**

Then ask:

> **“How would I explain that to a pharmacy owner I already know?”**

The first draft should sound closer to a useful conversation than to finished marketing copy.

Only after that should the writer improve clarity, rhythm, accuracy and platform fit.

---

## 3. What the historical Titan writing teaches us

Performance analysis and manual review of Titan captions point in the same direction.

### 3.1 Specific beats abstract

Strong Titan captions repeatedly lead with real people, places, numbers, scenes, outcomes or recognisable pharmacy problems.

Examples of concrete language:

- 16,000 items
- six weeks off
- an empty picking column
- 8pm
- a named pharmacy
- a GTIN
- a Meditech robot
- five spokes

Weak writing often substitutes abstract marketing language:

- streamlined
- seamless
- scalable
- smarter
- ecosystem
- enhanced
- efficient
- solution

These words are not banned. They should normally be the conclusion the reader reaches after seeing the evidence, not the evidence itself.

**Prefer:** “one hub designed to support five spokes”  
**Over:** “a scalable dispensing solution”.

### 3.2 Short sentences are seasoning, not the meal

Titan’s strongest openings often use short lines. Strong captions do not stay in headline mode for the entire post.

Avoid writing every sentence as:

> One login.  
> One workflow.  
> One ecosystem.  
> Less admin.  
> More care.

Open sharply when the idea earns it, then return to normal speech.

### 3.3 Observation beats manufactured persuasion

Strong Titan writing often sounds like somebody noticed something.

Examples of the underlying move:

- “Look familiar?”
- “Then you walk through the back.”
- “Living it is something else.”
- “Genuinely curious.”

The writer is present in the caption without constantly talking about Titan.

### 3.4 Let the reader complete the thought

Do not explain the moral of every story.

Weak pattern:

> “The interesting bit isn’t the technology. It’s what the technology enables.”

Better:

> “As the group grows, they don’t want to recreate the same dispensing workload five times.”

The second version shows the logic. The reader lands on the insight themselves.

**Rule:** show the logic; do not announce the insight.

### 3.5 Human writing contains useful “unnecessary” sentences

AI often removes the lines that give copy character because they do not advance the sales argument.

Do not automatically remove:

- dry asides
- understatement
- a slightly odd observation
- a sentence used mainly for rhythm
- a genuinely curious question
- a small human qualifier

Examples of the function, not templates:

- “Genuinely curious.”
- “Living it is something else.”
- “Comes with coffee (obviously).”

A caption should be concise, but not sterilised.

### 3.6 Understatement beats hype

Prefer confidence that does not announce itself.

**Prefer:** “A useful example of hub and spoke taking shape in Wigan.”  
**Avoid:** “A groundbreaking new model transforming the future of community pharmacy.”

### 3.7 Questions must be real questions

Do not ask a question merely because questions can drive engagement.

Weak:

> “Are you ready to transform your pharmacy?”

Better:

> “How are pharmacy teams actually using ChatGPT?”

A good question creates a genuine knowledge gap, invites an answer the writer actually wants, or challenges an assumption.

### 3.8 Second-person language is not the default

Avoid routinely telling pharmacy owners what their problem is:

> “Are you struggling with…?”  
> “Do you want to…?”  
> “Is your pharmacy…?”

Recognition is stronger when the reader recognises themselves in a scene, fact or observation without being instructed to.

### 3.9 Quotes make the writing inhabited

When a customer or expert has already said the strongest line, use it.

Do not polish authentic speech until it sounds like marketing copy.

A strong direct quote can carry Connection, Proof and voice at the same time.

### 3.10 Endings do not need a moral

A Titan caption may end on:

- a fact
- a quote
- an observation
- a useful question
- a slightly unresolved thought
- a simple line about what happens next

Do not automatically append:

> “And that’s why…”  
> “Ultimately…”  
> “The result?”  
> “This is what the future looks like.”

Trust the reader.

## 4. Titan’s voice repertoire

Titan does not need one voice. It needs a small repertoire of characters that all feel credible coming from the same account.

The writer must select the dominant voice before drafting.

### 4.1 The Observer

**Mental posture:** “I saw something worth pointing out.”

**Best for:** site visits, intake, multi-image posts, pharmacy operations, go-lives, visual proof.

**Traits:** specific, understated, curious, lightly editorial, concrete.

**Good opening territory:**

- a physical detail
- a surprising operational fact
- a contrast visible in the photos
- what is taking shape

**Avoid:** congratulatory corporate language and generic “great day at…” copy.

### 4.2 The Storyteller

**Mental posture:** “You need to hear what happened to this person.”

**Best for:** hero case studies, owner transformation, personal stakes, before/after stories.

**Traits:** human, narrative, patient, evidence-rich, emotionally restrained.

**Good opening territory:**

- a turning point
- a moment of tension
- a before/after contrast
- a quote with stakes

**Avoid:** making Titan the protagonist.

**Principle:** the pharmacy owner is the protagonist; Titan is the enabler.

### 4.3 The Industry Mate

**Mental posture:** “Have you seen this? There’s something interesting going on here.”

**Best for:** reactive news, expert calls, regulation, market changes, Hooman/Tariq/Wahid/Jeff commentary.

**Traits:** conversational, informed, genuinely curious, not over-produced, comfortable with nuance.

**Good opening territory:**

- a live industry development
- a question people are actually discussing
- an observation that changes how the news should be read

**Avoid:** pretending every news item is a crisis or an opportunity for Titan.

### 4.4 The Playful Insider

**Mental posture:** “We both know pharmacy can be a bit ridiculous.”

**Best for:** TikTok, pharmacy culture, native entertainment, occasional lighter LinkedIn posts.

**Traits:** knowing, dry, relatable, culturally specific, occasionally cheeky.

**Good opening territory:**

- recognisable pharmacy behaviour
- POVs
- small absurdities
- visual jokes
- deadpan observations

**Avoid:** generic internet humour that could come from any brand.

### 4.5 The Broadcaster

**Mental posture:** “This is worth your time.”

**Best for:** event talks, keynotes, expert clips, useful explainers, important announcements.

**Traits:** clear, assured, low-hype, concise.

**Good opening territory:**

- why this talk matters
- the useful idea inside it
- the person worth hearing from

**Avoid:** “we’re delighted/excited/proud” event filler.

### 4.6 The Challenger

**Mental posture:** “The industry may be looking at this the wrong way.”

**Best for:** sector theses, campaigns, advocacy, strong expert opinion, pharmacy economics, robotics, hub and spoke, future-of-pharmacy arguments.

**Traits:** clear point of view, evidence first, intellectually provocative rather than aggressive.

**Good opening territory:**

- a real assumption worth challenging
- a business question with consequences
- a contradiction exposed by evidence

**Avoid:** outrage bait or declaring a consensus where none exists.

### 4.7 The Customer

This is not a house voice; it is a source voice.

When the customer’s wording is more memorable than ours, get out of the way.

Do not convert an authentic customer phrase into polished corporate English unless clarity or compliance requires it.

## 5. Hook system: creative territories, not formulas

Do not ask only “what is the best hook?”

First identify the most interesting territory in the source.

Possible territories include:

1. **Physical proof** — the thing we can actually see.
2. **Operational insight** — what changed in the way the pharmacy works.
3. **Human tension** — what was difficult, at stake or emotionally meaningful.
4. **Industry argument** — the assumption this example challenges.
5. **Analogy** — a genuinely useful comparison that makes a complex idea easier to grasp.
6. **Specific outcome** — a number or concrete result that creates immediate stakes.
7. **Quote** — somebody has already said the hook better than the brand can.
8. **Curiosity** — a question whose answer is not obvious to the intended audience.

### Hook quality test

Before publishing, ask:

- Does the hook contain a real thought, or merely announce the subject?
- Is the answer already obvious to the audience?
- Is it specific enough to be recognisably Titan/pharmacy?
- Does it create a payoff the body can actually deliver?
- Does it sound like a person would say it?
- Is it the same rhetorical skeleton as one of the last five posts?

If the answer to the last question is yes, deliberately search for a different voice or territory.

---

## 6. Analogy rules

Analogy is welcome. Manufactured metaphor is not.

A useful analogy should do one of three things:

- make a complicated operation easy to picture
- expose an assumption
- sharpen an argument

Bad analogy:

> “A pharmacy is an orchestra and Titan is the conductor.”

It decorates the sentence without explaining anything.

Better analogy territory:

> comparing duplication across multiple pharmacy branches with how other multi-site businesses centralise repeatable operations.

### Rules

- Treat analogies as analogies, not literal operational claims.
- Fact-check any factual statement embedded in an analogy.
- One analogy is enough.
- Do not generate one just because the caption needs “flavour”.
- Tariq’s established analogies should retain their attribution/voice rules from the Titan voice canon.

## 7. Feature lists and bullets

Feature lists are not inherently weak.

They become weak when the list is expected to create desire by itself.

### Feature list as explanation — allowed

After the reader already cares, bullets can make a complicated setup easier to understand.

For example:

> The operation brings together:
> - peer-to-peer hub-and-spoke workflow
> - automated storage and picking
> - one central dispensing operation
> - capacity for multiple spokes

### Feature list as persuasion — usually avoid

> Titan PMR ✅  
> Automation ✅  
> Hub and spoke ✅  
> Clinical services ✅

The second form resembles brochure copy unless the format itself is intentionally playful.

## 8. Emoji, hashtag and punctuation behaviour

These are packaging decisions, not voice.

### Emojis

- Default: 0–2.
- Use 3 only when the format naturally earns it.
- Do not decorate every paragraph.
- An emoji may act as a visual beat, joke or directional cue. It should not substitute for tone.

### Hashtags

- Default: 2–4 relevant hashtags at the end.
- Prefer sector/topic relevance over generic reach tags.
- Do not stuff hashtags into body copy.
- Keywords in the actual writing matter more than a hashtag wall.

### Ellipses

- Use only for a natural pause, hesitation or comic beat.
- Never use `...` as a generic suspense machine.

### Bullets

- Use when they improve comprehension.
- Do not force every informational post into a tick-box list.

## 9. AI anti-pattern blacklist

The following constructions are not absolutely banned, but an AI writer must treat them as high-risk and justify their use.

- “It’s not X. It’s Y.”
- “The interesting bit isn’t X…”
- “The best part?”
- “The result?”
- “Less X. More Y.”
- “One X. One Y. One Z.”
- “More X. More Y. More Z.”
- “This isn’t just X. It’s Y.”
- “Ultimately…” as a closing moral
- “At Titan, we believe…” as an opener
- “Here’s why that matters…” when the next sentence could simply show why
- generic “Are you struggling with…?” hooks
- generic “What do you think?” CTAs
- “transform”, “revolutionise”, “game-changing”, “seamless”, “innovative” without evidence

### QA test

Search the draft for symmetry.

If multiple sentences have the same grammatical shape, intentionally break one.

Search for abstract nouns/adjectives.

For each one, ask whether a number, scene, quote or concrete noun could do the job instead.

## 10. The conversational drafting method

This is the preferred AI drafting workflow.

### Step 1 — Read the source only

Do not write yet.

Extract:

- the strongest true detail
- the reason a pharmacy owner might care
- any number
- any quote
- any tension
- any unusual visual detail
- what is still unknown

### Step 2 — Choose a dominant Reason-to-Care Marker

Which of Craft, Value, Connection or Proof is doing the most work?

Identify any secondary marker.

Do not attempt to visibly mention the markers in the caption.

### Step 3 — Choose a voice

Observer, Storyteller, Industry Mate, Playful Insider, Broadcaster or Challenger.

### Step 4 — Explain it conversationally

Write 1–3 paragraphs as if answering:

> “What’s interesting about this?”

Do not write a social caption yet.

### Step 5 — Find the hook inside the explanation

The best hook is often already hiding in the natural explanation.

Do not generate 20 generic hook formulas unless creative exploration is specifically requested.

### Step 6 — Edit for platform

Improve:

- first two lines
- readability on mobile
- factual precision
- paragraph breaks
- unnecessary repetition

Do not remove personality merely to make the copy tighter.

### Step 7 — Anti-formula pass

Check for:

- reversal templates
- parallelism
- over-neat list structures
- forced CTA
- forced moral
- product inserted too early
- generic marketing words

### Step 8 — Recent-caption collision check

Compare with the last five relevant Titan captions.

If the same opening shape, transition or ending repeats, rewrite using another voice or hook territory.

### Step 9 — Read aloud

If the conversational explanation from Step 4 sounds more human than the caption, move the caption back toward the explanation.

## 11. How this system works with Titan’s content engine

### Intake

Default voices: Observer, Storyteller, occasionally Playful Insider.

The colleague’s job is to capture truth, not write marketing copy.

Look for:

- what changed
- why it changed
- what the pharmacy was struggling with
- what is physically interesting
- what number makes the story real
- what the owner/team actually said

### Events

Default voices: Broadcaster, Industry Mate, Observer, Playful Insider.

Different event outputs should sound different. A keynote clip should not sound like a dinner photo album.

### Case studies

Default voices: Storyteller, Customer, Challenger.

Human story first. Product proof enters when it explains the transformation.

### Native entertainment

Default voice: Playful Insider.

Some posts need no explicit Titan product reference. Account ownership and cultural relevance can do the brand work.

### Editorial intelligence

Default voices: Industry Mate, Challenger, Broadcaster.

The job is not to repeat news. It is to interpret what happened, why it matters and what a pharmacy owner might think or do differently.

### Product launches and demos

Default voices: Observer, Broadcaster, occasionally Challenger.

Start from a user problem or real workflow, not a feature list.

Preferred demo framing:

> recognisable problem → show the workflow → show the useful consequence

Internal product teams must supply accurate briefs and product truth. The content writer should not infer launches from informal conversations.

## 12. Hard rules for AI writers

1. Never publish a caption simply because content exists.
2. Never turn the Reason-to-Care Markers into visible copy structure.
3. Never make Titan the protagonist of a customer story.
4. Never invent a tension, result, quote, number, comparison or product capability.
5. Never smooth authentic speech into generic testimonial language without need.
6. Never force a CTA.
7. Never force a metaphor.
8. Never force a reversal.
9. Never assume concise means stripped of character.
10. Never write five consecutive captions from the same rhetorical skeleton.
11. Never use a second-person problem hook by default.
12. Never use product adjectives where evidence can make the point.
13. Always distinguish what is observed from what is inferred.
14. Always preserve British English.
15. Always check claims against approved Titan product knowledge and source material.
16. If a draft sounds more natural when explained in chat, rewrite it closer to the chat explanation.

## 13. Quality checklist before approval

### Content

- Why would a pharmacy owner care?
- Which Reason-to-Care Marker is genuinely strong?
- What is the strongest piece of evidence?

### Voice

- Which voice is speaking?
- Does the caption sound like that voice consistently?
- Is there a specific observation rather than generic messaging?
- Does it contain any useful human texture?

### Structure

- Does the hook contain a real thought?
- Is the answer to the hook too obvious?
- Does the body deliver the hook’s promise?
- Does the caption breathe after the opening?
- Does the ending need to explain itself, or can it simply stop?

### Anti-AI

- Is there an “It’s not X, it’s Y” construction?
- Is there excessive parallelism?
- Is every sentence suspiciously polished?
- Are abstract marketing words doing work that evidence should do?
- Does this resemble one of the previous five captions too closely?

### Accuracy

- Are all numbers sourced?
- Are product capabilities supported?
- Are legal/regulatory claims current?
- Are people, pharmacies and partner names correct?

---

## 14. What success looks like

A successful Titan caption should feel as though it could only have been written because somebody close to community pharmacy saw, heard or understood something worth sharing.

It may be funny, serious, challenging, useful or quietly observational.

It should not feel as though the brand selected a template.

The long-term aim is familiarity without repetition:

> **The audience recognises the mind behind Titan’s content, not the formula behind Titan’s captions.**
