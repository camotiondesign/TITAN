# Trustpilot Linguistic Analysis for Titan PMR

Primary research from 200 Trustpilot reviews, 13 September 2024 to 6 August 2026. Written to shape content strategy, not to describe it.

Scope limitation: only the reviews visible on Trustpilot's public feed at the time of scraping are included. Older reviews sitting behind the Trustpilot login wall (and some paywalled or hidden variants) are not in this dataset.

Sample: 197 five star, two four star, one one star. Average length 75 words. Median 55 words. 107 reviews longer than 50 words. 144 reviews (72 per cent) name at least one Titan staff member. Date span covers late 2024 launch momentum through the summer 2026 face to face training push.

---

## Executive summary

Customers do not describe Titan the way Titan describes Titan. They describe Nas.

The single loudest fact in the data is that Nas (also spelled Naz, Nasim, Nas Khaliq) is mentioned by name in 84 of the 200 reviews. That is 42 per cent of the total sample and roughly three and a half times the volume of the next named person (Atique, 25). Nour, Maj, Nabil, Vicky, Dan, Ashley, Sarah and Saj carry the rest. In seventy two per cent of reviews the customer names at least one human. This is not a product review site. It is a thank you card wall for the implementation team.

The second loudest fact is that customer vocabulary and Titan marketing vocabulary barely overlap. Customers say "helpful", "patient", "amazing", "brilliant", "confident", "team", "training", "transition", "smooth". Titan's shop window says "AI", "ecosystem", "innovation", "hub and spoke", "modern PMR", "batch flow", "clinical services". "Ecosystem" appears zero times in customer speech. "Hub" appears zero times. "AI" appears three times. "Innovation" appears four. "Automation" appears three. "Workflow" (16) and "efficiency" (30) survive, but even these are usually plumbing words, not headline words. If Titan quoted its customers back at them, most of the current homepage copy would have to go.

The dominant emotional arc is a small four beat story. The customer was on a legacy PMR for 15, 20, 35 years. They were nervous, warned, dreading it. A named person from the Titan team held their hand through it. They now cannot imagine going back. This story appears again and again with different names in the second beat and different pharmacy sizes in the fourth. It is the story to tell.

The functional payoff is not "efficiency" in the abstract. It is Anna Matthews leaving on time for the first time in a year. It is Ansar Ghafoor's staff getting eighteen hours a week back. It is a seventy year old dispenser who has never used a computer sitting in front of Titan and enjoying it. The emotional payoff is relief. Copy that sells efficiency will always underperform copy that sells relief.

The single biggest content opportunity in the next ninety days is turning the "held our hand" arc into the campaign spine. It is native to how customers already speak. It counters the number one hidden objection (fear of the switch). And it puts named humans at the centre, which is the only thing this Trustpilot page proves people care to write about.

### 1.4 The verbs that do the work

Customers reach for a small palette of verbs and it is worth respecting them.

- Guide, walk, show, explain, break down, cover, run through
- Hold, look after, support, help, aid, assist
- Free up, save, streamline, cut down, reduce
- Feel, get used to, get to grips with, pick up
- Transform, change, revolutionise, modernise (the last two are rare)

Note that the "help" verbs are gentle. Nobody says the team "onboarded" them or "deployed" them or "provisioned" them. They were "guided", "walked through", "held". That is the vocabulary of a nervous customer being reassured by a competent stranger.

### 2.3 Relief as the dominant emotion

"Complete and utter relief" (Anna Matthews, 2026-02-28) is the highest emotional watermark in the sample and it deserves attention. Almost every other five star review is essentially describing relief in different vocabulary.

"Life changer" (David Lloyd, 2025-06-03, and Amy, 2024-12-10, and Harry, 2024-10-13). "Better than sliced bread" (Ansar Ghafoor, 2025-01-30). "Not looking back", "no looking back", "I would not go back": at least four separate reviews use the never-going-back framing, which is relief expressed as a permanent commitment.

The takeaway is that "efficient", "modern", "innovative" are what marketers see. Relief is what the customer feels. Content that leads with the feeling will pull harder than content that leads with the feature.

Customers do not open with the product. They open with the person, or with the experience.

- "Nas was our Titan trainer." (Suzanne Williams, 2026-08-05)
- "Great training for Titan PMR by Nas" (Julie Mccormack, 2026-08-05)
- "Nabil was excellent and very patient" (phanikumar pilli, 2026-08-01)
- "Had a fab training morning with Nas" (Emma, 2026-07-29)
- "Recently trained in titan PMR by Nas." (Helena, 2026-07-28)
- "Titan PMR is revolutionary." (Dan, 2026-07-16)
- "Fantastic service from day one!" (Louis Chapman, 2026-07-12)
- "We have been changing our PMR system over to TITAN over the last few weeks." (Orchards Pharmacy, 2026-07-09)
- "Titan set up has been great so far." (Rahul Sharda, 2026-07-04)

The most common opening pattern is a subject-verb-adjective sentence of five to eight words followed by a comma or full stop. It is essentially a headline followed by explanation. That is a caption structure Titan can borrow directly.

### 4.2 How they close

Closes are almost always one of three forms.

The thank you close.
"Thank you again, Nabil, Nas, Jeff, and Saj" (Mrs A Smarandache, 2026-03-25)
"Thank you all" (Moj Salehi, 2025-07-24)
"Thanks again Nas!!" (Jagdeep Jalf, 2025-06-17)

The recommendation close.
"I’d definitely recommend Titan PMR and the Titan CD Register to other pharmacies looking for a simpler way to manage their CD records" (Pritpal Grewal, 2026-06-26)
"I would highly recommend it to any pharmacy looking for a modern, efficient, and well-supported PMR system." (Ricky Singh, 2026-03-04)
"If you're having it installed, I'd recommend doing the following" (Anna Matthews, 2026-02-28)

The signature close.
"Louis @ Orchards Pharmacy" (Louis Chapman, 2026-07-12)
"Muhammad Alam." (Mehboob Aujla, 2026-04-11)
"Olu (Swinford Pharmacy)" (Mavenpharmacy Limited, 2025-05-04)
"Gulam Nabi Woodend Pharmacy, Coventry" (Gulam Nabi, 2025-03-13)

The signature close is worth pausing on. Pharmacists write their pharmacy name into reviews the way a shopkeeper signs a Christmas card. The pharmacy identity is fused to the personal identity in a way that is quite specific to this industry. Titan's content should respect this by naming the pharmacy in customer stories, not just the pharmacist.

### 4.3 Rhythm and length

The average review is 75 words but the median is 55. There is a distinct cluster of very short reviews (fewer than 20 words, 15 of them) and a long tail of substantial ones (107 above 50 words). The very short reviews are a valuable resource because they are essentially pre-written LinkedIn hooks.

"Nas was the best trainer!!" (Morgan Giddings, 2026-07-22, 5 words)

"The best PMR system on the market! A smooth onboarding experience thanks to Maj. Highly recommended!" (York Delivery Pharmacy, 2025-10-27, 15 words)

"Nas is very helpful" - "She really helped us to move forward and very positive. Many thanks to her support" (Arash Nava, 2026-01-02, 20 words)

"Nas was extremely helpful and friendly. She made the transition run smoothly and efficiently." (Lisa, 2026-01-14, 13 words)

"Best pmr going, support is amazing just a phone call away." (Adal Bashir, 2025-03-10, 10 words)

The long form reviews follow a similar internal shape. Situation, initial fear, named implementation lead, what they specifically did, how the pharmacy feels now, what it means for patients or staff, close with a thank you or recommendation. It is a reliable five beat structure and works for a long form LinkedIn caption as well.

### 4.4 What they use instead of prose

Emojis appear naturally but never densely. Stars in text, praying hands, medals, exclamation marks. Anna Matthews's review is a masterclass in emoji as punctuation. Most reviews use one or two. British customers do not lean on emojis for meaning, they use them like commas.

Exclamation marks appear 142 times across the corpus, averaging 0.7 per review. All caps words appear 220 times, mostly for TITAN itself or for names. Ellipses appear 59 times, often mid sentence, betraying that many reviews were tapped out on a phone in the pharmacy after a long day.

## Section 5: Customer worldview

The set of beliefs that come off the page again and again, each one evidenced.

### 5.1 Changing a PMR is never easy

This is the single most repeated framing device in the corpus.

"a change of PMR which is never easy!" (Rahul Sharda, 2026-07-04)
"Changing systems in pharmacy is always a challenge as you have to do it whilst you’re working." (Nathaniel Mitchell Allison's C, 2026-04-18)
"Changing PMR systems is never easy, after years of using one software, moving to a new one truly feels like learning to walk all over again." (Sai Kishore Bejugam, 2026-03-20)
"Changing software in a pharmacy can often be a daunting task" (Plashet Pharmacy, 2025-02-16)

This is not a Titan problem. It is a pharmacy owner belief that Titan is fighting against every time it tries to sell. It is also the anchor for every persuasion move in the customer sales cycle.

### 5.2 The software matters, but the person matters more

Almost every long form review makes this explicit.

"The combination of an amazing system and top-tier customer service has made all the difference." (Naveed, 2026-02-25)
"Titan PMR’s user-friendly interface, combined with their unmatched support, has modernised the way we operate." (Ronak Manek, 2025-08-12)
"Thanks to Atique’s dedication and support, we feel we’re off to a great start with Titan PMR." (Kerning Pharmacy, 2026-02-27)

Customers do not evaluate PMR software in isolation. They evaluate the whole delivery. Titan has clearly staffed for this. Content should reflect it.

### 5.3 My time as pharmacist is the constraint

The word "time" appears 65 times. Almost every mention is about the pharmacist getting time back.

"Titan frees up the pharmacist(s) time to do services and see more patients." (Mavenpharmacy Limited, 2025-05-04)
"It frees up the pharmacist allowing them to provide more services to the community." (samit patel, 2025-07-01)
"it has freed up my time as a pharmacist to focus on patient care and private services!" (Safys Chemist & Travel Clinic, 2025-01-10)
"I can focus on my consultations without the dread of knowing I've got several hours of checking to do when we close." (Anna Matthews, 2026-02-28)

The customer belief here is that pharmacist time is the bottleneck in the business, and anything that returns pharmacist time will pay back everywhere else.

### 5.4 Community pharmacy is under existential pressure and software has to help us survive

Multiple reviews frame Titan not as an efficiency upgrade but as survival infrastructure.

"Something that can save community pharmacies with the NHS cut - It has to be TITAN" (Amit, 2025-10-22)
"Titan PMR is an essential tool for any pharmacy looking to modernize and boost productivity and their chances of survival in the current volatile landscape of community pharmacy. Efficiencies have become critical to our survival" (Harry at Harry's Pharmacy, 2024-10-13)

This is a much bigger frame than "software that saves time" and Titan is welcome to use it. It also carries a real risk: language that leans too far into "survival" reads as scare copy. The customers get away with it because they are living it. Titan needs to be careful.

### 5.5 Barcode scanning and workflow ordering are the safety net

Safety words appear steadily. "Errors" (10 reviews), "accuracy" (6), "safer/safety" (9), "mistakes" (implied). The mechanism customers reach for is barcode scanning and the workflow order.

"It undoubtedly has some failings, much of these centre around the system being too rigid and not open to different ways of utilising certain funcitons / processes" (Hassan Ali, 2025-10-02, one of the very few honest limitation notes)

"It ensures that the right medicine (& quantity) is dispensed (scanning of original boxes) and flags up any safety issues like interaction and others." (Mavenpharmacy Limited, 2025-05-04)

"Barcode scanning reducing errors to virtually nil." (Gulam Nabi, 2025-03-13)

"the margin for human error has been reduced to zero" (Lulu, 2024-12-11)

Note the language. Not "reduced errors". "Reduced to zero" and "virtually nil". Pharmacist psychology around errors is binary. There is no acceptable rate. Titan copy should respect that.

### 5.6 Staff morale improves when software works

A less obvious belief, but present. Staff engagement is treated as an outcome, not an input.

"All my dispensing staff enjoy using the system as it is extremely user friendly" (Amy, 2024-12-10)
"My team, who were nervous about switching, are completely reassured and excited about using it going forward." (Anna Matthews, 2026-02-28)
"Not only is staff morale up, we’re doing more work but with less effort." (Rahul Puri, 2025-04-02)
"Improved staff satisfaction, and enhanced patient service." (Ronak Manek, 2025-08-12)

This is a reframe worth borrowing. Software is normally sold on productivity. Customers here are saying it improves the day of the person using it. That is a warmer story.

## Section 6: Hidden objections

Ranked by frequency of appearance as pre-sale anxieties the reviewer named.

### 6.1 The switch is going to be painful and slow

Direct evidence.
"I was warned that changing PMRs is super stressful and to expect a nightmare few weeks getting to grips with it all" (Anna Matthews, 2026-02-28)
"Changing PMR systems is never easy" (Sai Kishore Bejugam, 2026-03-20)
"Change of PMR which is never easy!" (Rahul Sharda, 2026-07-04)
"this was a huge change for us and it wasn’t something I wanted to rush." (Nathaniel Mitchell Allison's C, 2026-04-18)

Answer in copy. Reframe the switch story from painful to guided. Use "held our hand" language. Show the actual timelines (some reviews mention 4-5 weeks to comfort, 7 days for urgent onboarding).

### 6.2 My staff will resist. My older staff especially

Direct evidence.
"Some of my team members (over 70 years of age) who never used computers are actually loving to work on titan." (Amit, 2025-10-22)
"My team, who were nervous about switching, are completely reassured" (Anna Matthews, 2026-02-28)
"He quickly got a feel for where we were in our training and adapted his support to each staff member’s level" (Pritpal, 2025-10-10)
"The training she provided was fantastic spending time with each colleague and even organising some virtual training for colleagues who were not working on the day." (Milan Mehta, 2025-09-10)

Answer in copy. Show the least confident staff member on the video. Not the pharmacist owner. Not the young dispenser. The dispenser who is nervous around computers.

### 6.3 I cannot afford to disrupt patient service

Direct evidence.
"I don't like limiting service provision, even for a day or two, because my business so heavily relies on it" (Anna Matthews, 2026-02-28)
"Change of ODS code happened with no disruption to patient service." (Ali Mansour, 2026-03-26)
"Jeff Shelley who worked overnight to ensure our change of ODS code happened with no disruption to patient service." (same, 2026-03-26)
"We didn’t experience any issues on our go-live day, which was a huge relief" (Kardo, 2025-09-24)

Answer in copy. Show the go-live day as a normal working day. Post an ODS switch story. Publish the specific things Titan does to protect patient service during migration.

### 6.4 The system will be too rigid and I will lose the workarounds I depend on

Direct evidence (this is the rarest to appear but the most honest).
"It undoubtedly has some failings, much of these centre around the system being too rigid and not open to different ways of utilising certain funcitons / processes" (Hassan Ali, 2025-10-02)

Answer in copy. Do not pretend this away. Acknowledge that Titan has opinions about how workflow should be structured, explain why, and show the payoff. The customer respected this even while flagging it.

### 6.5 I am a locum with access to multiple pharmacies. How do I actually use this?

The only one star review is entirely about this.

## Section 14: Writing style analysis

### 14.1 Voice

Direct. First person plural (we) or first person (I). Present perfect and simple past dominate. British spellings with the exception of a few US spellings from more recent reviewers ("revolutionize", "modernize"). Almost no hedging language. Almost no jargon.

### 14.2 Rhythm

Short sentences at the top of a review. Longer sentences in the middle. Short again at the close. This mirrors the natural rhythm of a spoken recommendation. It is a good template for LinkedIn captions.

### 14.3 Density

Adjective density is high early in a review ("brilliant", "fantastic", "amazing", "exceptional") and drops in the middle where the story is told. The praise is at the edges. The evidence is in the body.

### 14.4 Signature moves worth borrowing

- Naming the specific person then their role (e.g. Nas from Titan, Maj my trainer, Vicky from the implementation team)
- Naming the specific pharmacy at the end (e.g. Louis @ Orchards Pharmacy, Gulam Nabi Woodend Pharmacy Coventry)
- The confession opener ("I was warned that", "I was worried initially", "I was sceptical")
- The comparison move ("miles ahead", "leaps and bounds", "the best I have used in 15 years")
- The permission move ("if you're thinking about the switch")
- The number in the middle of a paragraph ("20 hours a week", "7 sites in 6 weeks", "over 20,000 items", "35 years")

### 14.5 Signature moves worth avoiding

- The word "revolutionary" is used five times but always by early customers or long form testimonial writers. It has an over promising ring. Use with care.
- Superlative pileups ("truly amazing", "absolutely fantastic", "incredibly outstanding") are frequent in Trustpilot voice but do not land the same way in native LinkedIn content. Cut for LinkedIn.
- Emojis. Sparingly.
- Long unbroken paragraphs. Customers get away with them because they are earnest. Titan's own content should not.

### 14.6 Emoji patterns

Emojis appear in about 25 of the 200 reviews. Most common: stars, praying hands, medals, tick, red heart, laughing face. Almost none are decorative. Each carries a small piece of meaning. Not the LinkedIn style of blue diamond bullet points. More like handwritten Christmas cards. If Titan uses emojis, use them the way customers do or not at all.
